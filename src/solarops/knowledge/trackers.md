# Single-axis trackers

## How trackers work

Most large Australian solar farms mount modules on single-axis trackers that rotate east to west during the day to follow the sun. Tracking raises energy yield compared with fixed-tilt arrays, mostly in the morning and afternoon. A tracking farm on a clear day produces a broad, flat-shouldered output curve rather than the rounded peak of a fixed-tilt farm.

## Backtracking

At low sun angles, rows of trackers would shade each other, so the controller rotates them back towards flat as the sun gets lower. This is called backtracking. It reduces row-to-row shading but means early-morning and late-afternoon output is lower than full tracking would give. Backtracking is normal behaviour and is one reason the performance index is not calculated at low irradiance.

## Wind and hail stow

Trackers move to a safe stow position when wind speed exceeds the manufacturer's threshold, and some sites also stow for forecast hail. A farm in wind stow produces noticeably less than the weather model expects, because the modules are no longer pointing at the sun. Output returning to normal as the wind drops, on a windy but sunny day, is the typical signature.

## Tracker faults

A stuck tracker row stays at a fixed angle while others move, so it produces well in part of the day and poorly in the rest. Common causes are motor or drive failures, controller faults, loss of power or communications to the row controller, and damaged or seized bearings. Faults affecting only a few rows cause small farm-level losses that are best found by comparing row angles in the tracker monitoring system.

## Tracker maintenance

Preventive maintenance includes checking fasteners, drives and bearings, lubricating where the design requires it, checking inclinometer calibration, and testing stow functions before the storm season. Controller firmware and stow thresholds should only be changed in line with the manufacturer's documentation.
